<div class="bluf"><span class="lbl">Bottom line up front</span><br>
A 50/50 company is a "deadlock" structure by design — neither party can outvote the other, so a disagreement can paralyse the business unless a resolution mechanism is agreed <b>in advance</b>. If the deal is an equity JV, these provisions are non-negotiable. (If it's an agency arrangement, this document does not apply.)</div>

## The deadlock problem

With equal ownership, every material decision needs genuine agreement. Without a pre-agreed tie-breaker, a single sustained disagreement can freeze the company — the single most common way 50/50 ventures fail.

## Standard mechanisms

| Mechanism | What it does |
|---|---|
| Reserved matters | Defines exactly which decisions need both parties' sign-off (vs. day-to-day autonomy) |
| Escalation | Named senior individuals attempt resolution before anything drastic |
| Mediation / expert determination | Neutral third party resolves specific disputes |
| Buy-sell ("shotgun") clause | Last resort: one party offers to buy/sell at a set price, forcing a clean resolution |

## Exit provisions

<div class="callout"><span class="lbl">Plan the ending at the beginning</span><br>
Agree upfront: how either party sells their stake, what happens to customers and IP on exit, valuation method, and any drag/tag rights. Ventures that document the exit while relations are good avoid the worst disputes when they aren't.</div>

## Boundary

These are standard structures, not drafted terms. A UK corporate solicitor experienced in cross-border JVs must draft the actual provisions (see Solicitor_Brief). Do not proceed with an equity JV that lacks a deadlock mechanism.

## Implication

If the CEO confirms an equity JV (Pack 22 Q1), these provisions move from "good practice" to "must-have before signing." Budget for proper legal drafting accordingly (Pack 16).
